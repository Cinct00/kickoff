# Researching a Topic

Research fails in two symmetrical ways: stopping at the first plausible answer, and wandering forever without converging. The cure for both is the same — treat research as a *structured descent*: map first, then dig, with a question sharp enough to tell you when you're done. The difference between an hour of skilled research and a week of unskilled browsing is mostly this structure, not effort.

## Write the question down first

Before searching anything, write the actual question — one or two sentences — and what a *sufficient* answer looks like: a decision made? a summary written? a working understanding? This is the research version of [[understanding-the-problem]], and it earns its keep twice: it exposes vague quests ("learn about AI regulation" → *for what?*) before they eat a week, and it gives you a stopping condition, without which research continues until exhaustion substitutes for completion. Expect the question to sharpen as you learn — that's progress, not failure; rewrite it when it does.

## Map the territory before digging anywhere

The beginner's error is starting with details — diving into the first article, which answers a question you can't yet evaluate from a perspective you can't yet place. Instead, spend the first session on *survey-grade* sources: encyclopedia entries, textbook chapters, review articles, "state of the field" pieces, a couple of well-regarded explainers. You're not after answers yet; you're after the **map**: what are the major subtopics, the main schools of thought, the key terms, the canonical sources everyone cites? Twenty minutes of map changes every subsequent hour from wandering into navigation (learning a skill works the same way).

The map's most valuable cargo is **vocabulary**. Every field compresses its knowledge into terms of art, and until you know that what you call "the thing where returns shrink" the field calls "diminishing marginal returns," you can't search, can't read efficiently, and can't tell experts from performers. Collect the terms as you skim; each one unlocks a shelf.

## Follow citations like a detective

The best sources are rarely found by searching — they're found by *following*:

- **Backward**: when a source makes a load-bearing claim, chase its citation. You'll simultaneously find the origin (often subtler than the retelling — [[evaluating-information]]) and discover the literature's spine, because everything cites the spine.
- **Forward**: given one good paper or book, find what cites *it* (citation indexes make this one click). This surfaces the current conversation, including the corrections and refutations that the original — naturally — doesn't mention.
- **Sideways**: identify the three-to-five names that keep appearing, and read them directly. Every field is smaller than it looks; finding its actual experts collapses months of secondhand reading — and distinguishes them from the *loud* voices, who are optimized for reach rather than rightness. The tell: experts hedge in calibrated ways and engage the counterarguments; performers are certain about everything.

## Find where the field disagrees

A topic isn't understood until you know its live debates. Every field has settled ground, contested ground, and open frontiers — and presenting contested claims as settled (or vice versa) is the signature error of shallow research. Ask directly: *what do serious people in this field disagree about, and why?* The disagreements are also your best evaluation instrument: reading one credible source from each side of a real debate teaches you more about the evidence landscape than five sources from the same camp — and inoculates you against mistaking one school's confident voice for the field's conclusion ([[evaluating-information]] — steelman, independent chains).

## Take notes as claims, and synthesize as you go

Copied passages and highlighted PDFs produce the illusion of capture; three weeks later they're a pile. Note instead in **claims with pedigrees**: what is asserted, by whom, on what evidence, and how confident you are — "Remote work cuts turnover ~20% (single 2022 self-report survey, n=1,400, vendor-funded — weak)" outperforms three paragraphs of quotation. Periodically stop collecting and **write a synthesis from memory**: the current answer to your question, in your own words, sources closed. This is retrieval practice doing double duty: it consolidates what you've learned and — more importantly — *exposes the gaps and contradictions* your notes were hiding. The gaps become your next search queries; this loop (collect → synthesize → find gaps → collect) is the engine of converging research.

## Timebox the descent, and know when to stop

Research has diminishing returns and no natural floor; impose structure on the descent: a survey pass (map, vocabulary, the question sharpened), a directed pass (the gaps, the debates, the primary sources that matter), and a verification pass (the load-bearing claims traced to origin). Budget each in advance, sized to the stakes — an afternoon total for a purchase, days for a strategic bet. **Stop when you can answer the question you wrote down, state the main counterargument fairly, and name what you'd investigate next if it mattered more.** That third item is the honest residue of all real research: not "I know everything," but "I know the shape of what I don't" — which is exactly what to hand to the decision this research was for ([[making-decisions]], [[technical-communication]]).
