# Creative Thinking

Creativity is not a personality type — it's a process with known mechanics, run by people who mostly deny being creative while running it. The core mechanic is almost embarrassingly simple: **new ideas are made from existing ideas, recombined** — and the reason most people produce few of them isn't a missing gift; it's that they judge each candidate to death at birth, feed the recombiner a thin diet, and quit before the good ones arrive. Fix those three, and output follows.

## Never generate and judge at the same time

The foundational discipline, and the one most violated: idea production and idea evaluation are opposing mental modes, and running them simultaneously — proposing an idea while pre-hearing its critique — strangles production silently. You feel it as "I've got nothing," when the truth is "I killed everything pre-verbally." Split the modes with a hard wall:

- **Divergent phase**: quantity is the *only* metric. Bad ideas are welcome — required, even, since they're stepping stones and combination-fodder ("bad" idea + "bad" idea = the good one, routinely). No evaluation, no feasibility, no "but how would we—". Not even praise, which is judgment in a party hat.
- **Convergent phase**: later — different sitting if possible — cold-eyed selection, merging, and pruning ([[writing-clearly]]'s draft/edit split is this same law in prose; [[making-decisions]] takes over once candidates exist).

Quantity precedes quality by mechanism, not accident: study after study on creative output finds the most-lauded work arrives amid the most *total* work — the same people producing the masterpieces produce the forgettable bulk. First ideas are almost always the *conventional* ones (that's why they came first — they're everyone's first ideas); the interesting territory starts around idea fifteen, past the point where most sessions stop. So set generation quotas, not quality bars: "thirty options, no judging" outperforms "find a great idea" every time it's tried.

## Constraints are fuel, not cage

The blank page is not freedom — it's an unsearchably large space with no gradient. Constraints collapse the space and *force the search off the beaten path*: the haiku's seventeen syllables, the startup's no-budget marketing, the dinner from only what's in the fridge. When stuck with too much freedom, **add constraints artificially** and watch ideas arrive: solve it with no money; in one day; for a single user; without the feature everyone assumes; as if it were a game; the opposite way ("how would we make this problem *worse*?" — inverting reliably surfaces what actually matters, [[first-principles-thinking]]). And when a real constraint chafes, try treating it as the design's centerpiece instead of its obstacle — the histories of art and engineering are substantially a list of limitations turned into signatures.

## Feed the recombiner from distant fields

If new ideas are recombinations, the breadth of your inputs is your creative ceiling. Ideas from *within* your field produce increments; the leaps come from **analogical distance** — the biologist's pattern applied to logistics, the jazz structure applied to meetings, the game mechanic applied to onboarding. Practical consequences: read outside your lane on purpose (the adjacent-field textbook, the biography, the trade magazine of an industry you'll never join); collect interesting patterns *before* you need them (a note system of "huh, that's a neat mechanism" pays compound interest — [[researching-a-topic]]); and when stuck, ask the transplant question directly: *who else has a problem shaped like this, and what do they do?* Restaurants handle demand spikes, hospitals triage, air traffic control sequences — someone has spent decades on your problem wearing a different costume.

## Load the problem, then leave it alone

The shower insight is real machinery, not folklore: focused attention searches *locally* (near the current framing), while diffuse, wandering attention makes the *distant* connections — but only about problems that have been deeply loaded first. The protocol has both halves and most people skip one:

1. **Load hard**: immerse in the problem to the point of frustration — the details, the constraints, the failed attempts. The frustration is the loading indicator, not a stop sign.
2. **Then genuinely disengage**: walk, shower, sleep, chop vegetables — something occupying-but-undemanding. Not the phone; the feed fills the wandering bandwidth the insight needed.

Incubation without loading is just a walk; loading without incubation is just grinding. Alternate deliberately — and keep capture within arm's reach always (a note app, a pocket card), because ideas arriving off-duty evaporate in minutes, and the capture habit is what turns a leaky mind into a compounding one.

## Prototype cheap, and steal like a craftsman

Two closing disciplines that turn generation into output:

- **Make the crappy version fast.** Ideas evaluated in the head are evaluated by imagination, which is a flattering liar in both directions. The sketch, the paper mock, the twenty-line script, the role-played pitch — an hour of crude reality outperforms a week of internal debate, and kills the doomed ideas *cheaply* while they're still cheap ([[scoping-and-estimation]]'s spike, wearing paint). Expect to throw it away; its output is the learning.
- **Steal transformationally.** Every creator begins in imitation — that's how the craft installs itself — and the ethics live in the transformation: copy one source and it's plagiarism; digest twenty and make something none of them would recognize as theirs, and it's called influence, which is how the entire history of everything has worked. Study what you love until you can see *why* it works, then apply the why to new material. Originality isn't creation from nothing — it's an unusual combination, honestly transformed, shipped despite the certainty that idea sixteen looked stupid at first. They all do. That's how you know you're past the conventional ones.
