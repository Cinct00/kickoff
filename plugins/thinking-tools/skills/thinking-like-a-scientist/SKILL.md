# Thinking Like a Scientist

Science is not a body of facts, a profession, or a vibe of rationality — it's a *method for being wrong in a detectable way*. Its core move is almost embarrassingly humble: state what you believe precisely enough that reality could contradict it, then arrange for reality to get its chance. Everything else — the controls, the statistics, the peer review — is scar tissue from centuries of discovering how good humans are at fooling themselves. And the first principle, the one Feynman put at the center: **you are the easiest person to fool.** The method exists because sincerity is not a safeguard.

## A hypothesis must be able to lose

The dividing line between a scientific claim and a mood is **falsifiability**: what observation, if you saw it, would make this false? "Our users love dark mode" is a mood; "dark mode users will retain 5%+ better over 30 days" can lose — and *because* it can lose, it can also meaningfully win. Claims that reshape themselves to survive any outcome ("the market fell — investors feared inflation; the market rose — investors shrugged off inflation") explain everything and predict nothing; they're not wrong, they're *not even wrong*, which is worse. Before testing anything, write the prediction down — specific, in advance, with a number where possible ([[debugging-systematically]] runs this exact loop on code; [[probabilistic-thinking]] adds the confidence level). The write-it-first discipline is what blocks the mind's favorite trick: adjusting the prediction after the result, then remembering itself as having been right.

## The control group is the whole invention

The deepest scientific insight isn't any fact — it's a question: **compared to what?** Everything improves-after-treatment: the sick recover (regression to the mean — they enrolled *because* they were at their worst), the coached improve (they'd have practiced anyway), the medicated feel better (expectation is pharmacologically active). Without a comparison group that got everything *except* the treatment, "it worked" is uninterpretable ([[evaluating-information]] asks this as a consumer; here you're the producer). The refinements exist because each one closed a self-deception loophole: **randomization** (so the groups differ only by treatment, not by who chose which — volunteers for the new thing differ systematically from decliners), **blinding** (so belief can't leak into outcomes or measurements — the assessor who knows which group is which finds what they expect, honestly), and **pre-registration** (deciding the analysis before seeing the data, because data sliced twenty ways always confesses to something — [[evaluating-information]]'s forking paths, from the builder's side).

Design cheap experiments in ordinary life the same way: the A/B test with a real control, the two-week trial with a written prediction, the one-variable change ([[debugging-systematically]]). Most "we tried it and it worked" stories in business are uncontrolled single-arm trials with enthusiastic assessors — which is to say, testimonials.

## Measurement has error bars, and so does everything downstream

No measurement is a number; every measurement is a number *plus uncertainty*, and forgetting the second half manufactures false discoveries. The instrument wobbles, the sample varies, the day differs — so a metric that moved 3% on a base that wobbles ±5% moved *zero* until proven otherwise. The working habits: know the noise floor before celebrating the signal (run the null experiment: measure with no change and watch how much movement is normal); distinguish **precision** (consistent) from **accuracy** (correct) — a scale that's reliably 2kg heavy is precise, wrong, and still useful for tracking *change*; and beware the metric replacing the construct — "learning" becomes "test scores," "health" becomes "weight," and the proxy quietly becomes the target ([[systems-thinking]] — Goodhart, in the lab).

## All models are wrong; the skill is knowing where

Science doesn't produce truth — it produces *models*: deliberately simplified maps that predict well within a domain and break outside it. Newton isn't "wrong" because Einstein; Newton is a magnificent approximation whose boundary conditions are now known. This is liberating once internalized: the question for any model — in physics, economics, nutrition, or your head — is never "is it true?" but **"where does it hold, how well, and what breaks it?"** ([[making-decisions]]' catalog: the map is not the territory — science is the discipline of *annotating the map with its known distortions*). It follows that a model earns its keep by *predicting*, not by explaining after the fact — explanation is cheap, prediction is expensive, and the exchange rate between them is the whole epistemic economy.

## How science actually progresses — messy, and self-correcting anyway

The textbook picture — hypothesis, experiment, conclusion, truth — describes almost no actual science. The reality: findings that don't replicate, incentives that reward novelty over rigor ([[systems-thinking]] — read any system by its incentives, including this one), publication bias filtering out the null results, and genuine expert disagreement lasting decades. Knowing this makes you a *better* consumer, not a cynic, because the self-correction is also real — it's just slow and statistical: single studies are weather; **replicated, converging evidence from independent groups is climate** ([[evaluating-information]] — independent chains). The practical calibration: trust fields in proportion to their feedback loops (physics predicts to twelve decimals; nutrition epidemiology wrestles confounders forever — they deserve different deference), trust *consensus of evidence* over any hero study, and treat "scientists were wrong before" as the system working — the interesting question is never whether science has erred, but what the current best evidence says and how strong it is.

The scientist's posture, portable to everything: hold beliefs at the strength of their evidence, seek the test that could break your favorite idea before reality administers it unscheduled, and count changing your mind as the method *succeeding* ([[probabilistic-thinking]] — updating is the point; [[from-knowledge-to-judgment]] — surprise is the flag that your model met the territory). Strong opinions, cheaply falsifiable, actually tested: that's the whole art, and it works outside the lab exactly as well as inside.
