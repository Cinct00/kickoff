# Making Decisions

Decision quality and outcome quality are different things — good decisions sometimes lose, and coin-flip gambles sometimes pay off. You can't control outcomes; you can control process. The process below is what consistently tilts the odds, and it starts with the most neglected question: how much process does *this* decision deserve?

## Size the decision first

Match deliberation to stakes × reversibility ([[architecting-solutions]] calls these one-way and two-way doors):

- **Reversible and small** — decide *now*, with whatever information is at hand. The cost of a wrong choice is less than the cost of deliberating. Most decisions live here, and treating them as bigger than they are is the most common decision-making failure: slow choices about lunch venues and fast ones about mortgages.
- **Reversible but meaningful** — decide quickly, but instrument the exit: what would tell you it's wrong, and by when? "Try it for a month, revisit if X" converts a scary choice into a cheap experiment.
- **Irreversible and large** — this is where the full toolkit below earns its cost. Slow down *proportionally*, not infinitely.

## Start from the outside view

Before analyzing your specific situation (the inside view), ask: **what usually happens to people who do this thing?** What fraction of restaurants survive five years? How often do two-year projects finish in two years? How do most acqui-hires feel three years later? This base rate is your starting estimate, and you should adjust from it only as far as your situation *demonstrably* differs — not as far as your enthusiasm suggests. The inside view knows your case in loving detail; the outside view knows how the story usually ends. When they disagree strongly, the outside view is right more often than feels fair ([[probabilistic-thinking]]).

## Run the pre-mortem

Before committing: *"It's one year later and this decision failed badly. What happened?"* Write the story — concretely, with mechanisms. This works because it flips the social and cognitive gradient: in planning mode, doubts feel disloyal and get suppressed; in pre-mortem mode, finding failure modes is the assignment, and the same brain that produced the rosy plan will cheerfully generate its obituary. Whatever the story names as cause of death — that's what to test, hedge, or fix before deciding, not after.

## Value information and options — but price them

- **Buying information**: if a cheap probe would change your choice, do it first — the pilot, the reference call, the weekend prototype ([[scoping-and-estimation]] calls this a spike). The test: *would* the answer actually change your decision? Information that can't change the choice is procrastination with a research budget.
- **Keeping options open** has real value and a real, usually hidden cost: while you preserve all paths, you advance down none, and the best options expire. "Deciding not to decide yet" is a decision — make it explicitly, with a date attached.

## Decide how you'll decide

- **Satisfice on the small stuff**: define "good enough" *before* looking, take the first option that clears it. Maximizing — searching for the best possible — burns time and, reliably, satisfaction: maximizers end up with better options and feel worse about them.
- **For values-laden choices** (career, relationships, where to live), spreadsheets underperform because the columns can't hold what matters. Better probes: the regret test (*at 80, which choice do I wish I'd made?*), the advice test (*what would I tell a friend in exactly my position?* — instantly strips the fear and sunk cost), and the body test (flip a coin, notice whether you hope for heads while it spins — you've already decided; the coin just delivered the news).
- **In groups**: separate the *decider* from the *input-givers* explicitly. Consensus produces beige compromises and three-month delays; a named decider who genuinely hears dissent, decides, and gets real commitment ("disagree and commit" — dissent recorded, sabotage off the table) produces decisions with edges. And write the decision down with its reasoning — or enjoy relitigating it quarterly ([[technical-communication]]).

## Know the classic saboteurs

- **Sunk cost**: money and years already spent are gone identically whether you continue or quit. Only the future is on the table. "We've invested so much" is the sound of good money saluting bad.
- **Analysis paralysis is usually fear** wearing diligence's badge — fear of owning the outcome. If the third analysis round is confirming the first, you're not learning, you're hiding. Decide.
- **Choice framing**: "should we do X?" invites yes/no on one option. Force a real alternative set — including the honest version of "do nothing," which is itself a choice with consequences and a base rate.

## Reach for the lens catalog

A small working set of mental models, each with the moment to reach for it. Don't run all of them; pick the two or three the situation calls for.

| Lens | Reach for it when | The move |
|---|---|---|
| **Inversion** | The goal is fuzzy or the plan feels too smooth | Ask "how would I guarantee this fails?" and avoid those things. The pre-mortem is inversion applied. |
| **Opportunity cost** | Something looks good in isolation | Compare it to the best thing you'd do with the same time or money, not to doing nothing. |
| **Second-order effects** | The first consequence is obviously good | Ask "and then what?" twice ([[systems-thinking]]). Incentives and responses arrive later than results. |
| **Margin of safety** | The downside is severe or estimates are shaky | Leave a buffer big enough that being wrong doesn't ruin you: budget, schedule, savings, capacity. |
| **Chesterton's fence** | Removing a rule, process or piece of code you don't understand | Find out why it's there before taking it down ([[refactoring-and-legacy-code]]). |
| **Base rates** | You're sure your case is special | Start from what usually happens, then adjust only for proven differences. |
| **Reversibility** | Choosing how much process to spend | One-way doors get rigour; two-way doors get speed. |
| **Expected value with ruin** | Weighing a risky bet | Probability × payoff, but rule out any option with a real chance of unrecoverable loss, however good the average looks ([[probabilistic-thinking]]). |
| **Regret minimisation** | A values-laden choice with no clear numbers | Pick the option your 80-year-old self is least likely to regret. |
| **Circle of competence** | Deciding in an unfamiliar field | Know where your judgment is reliable. Outside it, borrow an expert's or buy information first. |
| **Hanlon's razor** | Someone else's action looks hostile | Assume mistake, missing context or misaligned incentives before malice. |
| **Occam's razor** | Competing explanations | Prefer the simplest one that fits all the facts, then test it. |

**Checklist for a decision that deserves one:**
1. Is it a one-way or a two-way door? Set the time budget to match.
2. What are the real options, including "do nothing" and "wait until a set date"?
3. What's the base rate for this kind of choice?
4. Pre-mortem: a year on, it failed. Why?
5. What cheap probe would change my mind, and is it worth running first?
6. What's the worst case, and can I survive it?
7. Who decides, and who only gives input?
8. Write the decision, the expected outcome, my confidence, and what would show I was wrong.

## Keep a decision journal

For consequential calls, write at decision time: the situation, the options, what you expect to happen and with what confidence, and what would make you wrong. Review it when outcomes land. This is the only reliable defense against hindsight bias — the mind that "knew it all along" — and it's how you find *your* systematic tilts (too optimistic on timelines? too conservative on people?). Feedback is how judgment compounds; without the journal, you're practicing without a scoreboard ([[learning-anything]]).
