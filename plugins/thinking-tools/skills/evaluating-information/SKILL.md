# Evaluating Information

You can't verify everything — the volume is unpayable. What you can do is triage well: know which claims to accept provisionally, which to check, and *how* to check the ones that matter. The skill rests on one habit above all: **noticing when a claim is doing work** — about to change what you believe, decide, or repeat — and raising scrutiny exactly then, especially when the claim flatters what you already wanted to think.

## Trace it to the primary source

Most information arrives pre-chewed: a tweet about an article about a press release about a study. Each hop adds simplification, drama, and error — the study found a 12% correlation in mice; the headline says the food kills you. For any claim that matters, walk the chain backward to the primary source (the study, the filing, the transcript, the dataset) and check two things: *does it actually say what the retelling says?* (astonishingly often, no) and *does the primary source itself have a basis, or is it an assertion in a nicer font?*

Beware the echo that impersonates confirmation: five outlets reporting the same claim usually means one source and four rewrites — and sometimes the citation chain is perfectly circular (a fabricated fact gets cited, the citations become the evidence). Corroboration means **independent** chains, not repeated ones. Two genuinely independent sources agreeing is strong; fifty copies of one source is one source with a fan club.

## Read the incentives before the content

Every message has a sender, and every sender has a stake. This isn't cynicism — it's the first sorting question: *what does the source gain if I believe this?* The vendor's benchmark, the activist's statistic, the researcher's press release, the influencer's "honest review" — all can be true, but each is *selected*: of all the things the sender could have told you, this served them best. Incentive-reading also calibrates trust upward: a claim *against* the speaker's interest ("our product is the wrong fit for you") earns extra weight precisely because it's expensive to say ([[probabilistic-thinking]] — weigh evidence by how surprising it'd be if false).

Special flag for claims optimized to spread: outrage and wonder are selection pressures, not truth signals. The versions of a story that survive to reach you are the *most shareable* mutations, not the most accurate — assume viral means selected-for-virality until verified. "Too good to check" is precisely the feeling of a claim that needs checking.

## Run the statistical gauntlet

A handful of sleights account for most numerical deception; check them like a pilot's walkaround:

- **Relative vs absolute risk.** "Doubles your risk!" — from 1-in-10,000 to 2-in-10,000. Always demand the absolute numbers; relative risk without a baseline is theater.
- **What's the denominator?** "10,000 incidents!" — out of how many total? A count without a base is a mood.
- **Compared to what?** "90% success rate" means nothing without the alternative's rate — placebo, competitor, doing nothing.
- **Correlation ≠ causation, with teeth.** Ice cream sales correlate with drownings (summer causes both). For any correlation, run the three alternates: reverse causation? Common cause? Selection artifact? Only when all three fail does causation deserve the floor.
- **Statistically significant ≠ big.** Significance says "probably not zero," not "matters." Ask for the effect *size* — significant-but-trivial is a genre.
- **The garden of forking paths.** Slice data twenty ways and one slice "significant at p<0.05" is expected by chance. Was the hypothesis stated before the data, or discovered in it?
- **The chart's axes.** Truncated y-axes manufacture drama; log scales hide it; cherry-picked date ranges do the rest. Read the axes before the shape.

And for studies specifically: sample size ([[probabilistic-thinking]] — small samples scream), who was sampled (selection effects), who funded it, and whether it has ever been replicated. A single unreplicated study is a hypothesis with a press agent.

## Steelman before you dismiss

Scrutiny has a failure mode: applied only to disliked conclusions, it degenerates into sophisticated confirmation bias — the well-read are often *better* at explaining away inconvenient evidence, not worse. Two disciplines keep the tool honest:

- **Steelman**: before rejecting a claim, state its *best* version — the one its smartest advocate would give — and check that version. Refuting the dumbest version of an idea is aerobic exercise, not inquiry.
- **Symmetric standards**: notice when you demand peer review from opponents and accept anecdotes from allies. The tell is fluency — if you can't articulate what evidence *would* change your mind, you're defending, not evaluating.

## Hold conclusions at the strength of their evidence

The output of evaluation isn't "true/false" — it's a confidence level with a pedigree: *"probably true — primary source checks out, two independent confirmations"* or *"single vendor claim, unverified — treat as marketing until replicated."* Practice saying **"I don't know"** and **"I was wrong"** at conversational volume; both are load-bearing. And before repeating anything surprising, remember that repetition is endorsement: you become a link in exactly the chain this skill teaches you to distrust. The five minutes of checking isn't just for you — it's for everyone downstream of you ([[researching-a-topic]] for the deeper dig, [[technical-communication]] for reporting what you found honestly).
