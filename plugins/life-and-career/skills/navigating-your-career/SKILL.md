# Navigating Your Career

A career is a forty-year compounding system that most people steer with five minutes of thought a year — usually the five minutes after something bad happens. The alternative isn't a rigid ladder plan (those age like milk; the job you'll hold in fifteen years may not exist yet). It's managing the **portfolio that compounds**: skills, reputation, and relationships. Get those three accruing and the specific opportunities largely take care of themselves; neglect them and no amount of job-hopping optimizes you out of the hole.

## Compound the portfolio: skills, reputation, relationships

- **Skills**: optimize early-career choices for *learning rate* over salary delta — the knowledge compounds for decades, the few thousand dollars doesn't ([[personal-finance-principles]] — same math, different asset). Aim for a **T-shape**: one spike deep enough that people seek you out for it, on a base broad enough to collaborate across domains — and note that the *combination* is often the moat: merely-good engineering plus merely-good communication is rarer, and better paid, than excellence in either alone. When the learning curve flattens, that's a signal with a section below.
- **Reputation**: the industry is smaller than it looks, and it keeps minutes. The colleague of today is the hiring manager, reference, and co-founder of the next twenty years, and "does great work, easy to work with, keeps their word" compounds across companies in ways no résumé line does ([[persuasion-and-negotiation]] — the long game, career edition). Guard it accordingly: the shortcut that burns a relationship is almost never worth its price.
- **Relationships**: the useful network is not a LinkedIn number — it's people who've *seen your work* and would vouch for it. The research finding worth knowing: opportunities disproportionately arrive through **weak ties** — former colleagues, conference acquaintances — because your close circle knows the same openings you do. And the network is built by *giving first*: the introduction made, the question answered, the credit shared, years before any ask ([[systems-thinking]] — trust is a stock; fill it before you need to draw).

## Visibility is not vanity — unseen work doesn't exist

The quiet meritocratic hope — "great work speaks for itself" — is false in any organization larger than a room: work speaks only as far as it's *seen*, and the people deciding promotions and projects operate on what reaches them. This isn't an invitation to politics; it's a communication duty ([[technical-communication]]): ship the demo, write the summary post, present the postmortem, send your manager the monthly "what my work made possible" note — framed as impact ("cut onboarding from two weeks to two days"), not activity ("worked on onboarding"). Keep the **wins file** as you go ([[managing-yourself]] — it serves double duty against imposter feelings), because promotion cases and salary conversations are built from receipts, and receipts fade in everyone's memory including yours.

The companion move is **luck surface area**: luck isn't distributed randomly — it lands on people positioned in its path, and the position is *doing interesting work × telling people about it*. The talk given, the article written, the open-source contribution, the hallway conversation joined — each is a lottery ticket that costs almost nothing and occasionally pays a career. Most careers contain two or three lucky breaks; the deliberate part is maximizing the tickets held when the draws happen.

## Manage up: your manager runs on the information you supply

"Managing up" done honestly isn't flattery — it's recognizing that your manager is your highest-bandwidth channel to context, advocacy, and opportunity, and that the channel works exactly as well as you operate it. The mechanics: **no surprises** — bad news early is the entire relationship ([[technical-communication]]); bring options, not just problems ("here's the issue, here are two paths, I'd pick B — ratify or redirect" — you're demonstrating the judgment that promotions are made of); learn what *they're* measured on and connect your work to it; and **ask directly** for what you want — the stretch project, the promotion timeline, the raise ([[persuasion-and-negotiation]], and its salary playbook) — because managers reliably report the same lament: "I'd have advocated, but I didn't know they wanted it." Ambition unstated reads as contentment.

In performance conversations, seek the sharp version of the feedback: "what would make the difference between me and the next level?" — then treat the answer as the roadmap it is ([[difficult-conversations]] — receive it as data, mine the true fraction).

## Stay or go: decide on trajectory, not on a bad Tuesday

The honest stay/go audit runs on three questions: **Am I still learning?** (Not "is it comfortable" — comfort and growth trade off, and eighteen months of no new capability is the loudest signal there is — [[learning-anything]].) **Is there a path here?** — asked out loud to your manager; a vague answer *is* an answer. **Does the trajectory of this team/company lift my portfolio or cap it?** Rising environments hand you opportunities that stagnant ones can't manufacture.

The discipline is deciding from the *pattern*, not the incident ([[making-decisions]] — never decide in the trough of a bad week; also: the sunk-cost years are spent identically whether you stay or go). Run the outside view: people who leave in your situation — better or worse off in three years? And when you do go: **never burn the bridge on exit** — the two-week professionalism, the graceful handoff (a document like the one you're reading), the honest-but-warm goodbye. The industry keeps minutes, and exits are the most-read page. Counteroffers deserve particular skepticism: the raise that required a resignation letter answers the "is there a path here?" question retroactively, and the underlying reasons you looked elsewhere rarely change.

## Interviewing: the exam with known questions

An interview is a sample of evidence, not a verdict on your worth. Both sides have about four hours to predict years of working together, so the job is to make your evidence easy to read.

- **Prepare from the job, not from scratch.** Read the role description line by line and match each requirement to one story from your wins file. Research the company's product, stack and recent news so your questions are specific.
- **Behavioural rounds: tell stories with structure.** Use situation, task, action, result, and spend most of the time on *your* actions and the measurable result ("cut p99 latency from 40 ms to 6 ms"). Prepare six to eight stories that flex across common prompts: a hard technical problem, a conflict, a failure and what changed after, influencing without authority, a deadline under pressure, and a decision with incomplete information. Say "I", not "we", for what you did.
- **Coding rounds: think out loud.** Restate the problem, confirm inputs and edge cases, state the brute-force approach, then improve it. Narrate trade-offs as you go. A silent correct answer often scores below a talked-through near-miss, because the interviewer is grading how you reason. Test your code by hand before saying you're done ([[debugging-systematically]]).
- **System design rounds: drive the conversation.** Clarify requirements and scale first, sketch the end-to-end path, then go deep where the risk is: data model, bottleneck, failure modes ([[architecting-solutions]]). Name the trade-offs you're choosing; there is rarely one right design.
- **Ask questions that reveal the job.** "What does success look like in the first six months?" "What's the hardest problem the team has right now?" "How are decisions made when engineers disagree?" "Why is this role open?" The answers are your data for the stay-or-go call later.
- **Treat it as two-way.** Watch how they handle your questions, how interviewers talk about each other, and whether anyone mentions on-call load or attrition. A process that's disorganised now usually reflects the team.
- **After each loop, debrief yourself in writing.** Note which questions came up, where you were weak, and what to practise next ([[learning-anything]]). Rejections are noisy; a pattern across several is the signal.

When an offer arrives, stop interviewing mode and switch to negotiation ([[persuasion-and-negotiation]]). Don't accept on the call: thank them, ask for the full offer in writing, and set a date to respond.

## Drift is the default — steer annually

Careers mostly aren't wrecked; they're *drifted* — five years of the path of least resistance, arrived at nowhere in particular. The countermeasure is embarrassingly cheap: one honest hour per year ([[prioritization-and-focus]] — the weekly review, zoomed out). What did I learn this year — and is the portfolio compounding or coasting? What do I want more of, and what did I tolerate that I shouldn't? What's the one move — a skill, a conversation, a project bid, a search — that next year's review would thank me for? Write it down, diff it against last year's ([[making-decisions]] — the journal), and make the one move. A career steered one deliberate degree at a time ends up somewhere chosen; the unexamined one ends up wherever the current was going — and the current doesn't have your interests in mind.
