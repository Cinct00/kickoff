# Personal Finance Principles

Personal finance is a rare domain: the knowledge fits on an index card, has been stable for decades, and is drowned out by an industry whose revenue depends on convincing you it's complicated. The hard part was never the math — it's that the correct moves are *behaviorally* difficult (boring, delayed-gratification, contrarian under fire) while the wrong moves are exciting and immediate. So these principles are mostly about building a machine that runs without your daily willpower — because your daily willpower is exactly what the failure modes are designed to exploit. (These are reasoning principles, not advice for your specific situation — tax rules, account types, and personal circumstances vary; get local, personalized input for those.)

## Pay yourself first — by automation, not intention

The universal budgeting failure: spend, then save "what's left" — and discover, with iron reliability, that nothing is left. Spending expands to fill visible money ([[systems-thinking]] would call it a balancing loop defending your lifestyle). The fix inverts the order and removes the decision: **automatic transfer to savings/investments on payday, before the money is seeable**, with the remainder as guilt-free spendable. The percentage matters less than the automation at first — a machine moving 5% beats an intention of 20% — and the ratchet does the rest: raise the rate with every raise, *before* the lifestyle absorbs it. Lifestyle inflation is the silent killer of high incomes: earning triple and saving nothing is the standard biography, because spending grows to income unless something structural stops it. Capture half of every raise automatically and you'll never feel the loss of money you never met.

## Compounding is the whole engine — feed it time

Money growing on its own growth produces the least intuitive math in daily life: modest sums, given decades, beat large sums given years. The doubling arithmetic (roughly: 72 ÷ annual return ≈ years to double) means a sum at 7% doubles about every decade — so the dollar invested at 25 does *twice* the lifetime work of the one invested at 35. Three consequences most people learn too late:

- **Starting now beats starting bigger.** Time in the machine is the input that can't be bought back later at any price.
- **Interruptions are catastrophic, not proportional.** Cashing out mid-journey doesn't pause compounding; it amputates the biggest years, which are always the last ones.
- **The same math runs in reverse as debt.** High-interest debt *is* compounding, pointed at you — which is why paying it down is usually the best "investment" available at any given moment, and why minimum payments are engineered to make the hole self-deepening.

## Buy the market; stop paying for the dream of beating it

The evidence here is about as settled as social-science evidence gets: after costs, the overwhelming majority of professionals fail to beat simple broad-market index funds over long periods — and the few who do can't be identified *in advance* (past winners don't persist; you'd be picking last decade's lottery numbers). The retail investor's edge is refusing the game: **broad diversification, minimal fees, maximal boredom.** Fees deserve special hatred because they compound too, in reverse: a 1% annual fee sounds like nothing and quietly consumes a *quarter or more* of a multi-decade portfolio. Related disciplines: diversify for real (a portfolio of your employer's stock is your paycheck and savings betting on the same horse — [[probabilistic-thinking]] on correlated risks); never invest in what you can't explain to a smart teenager ([[evaluating-information]] — complexity in financial products is usually where the fees hide); and treat "guaranteed high returns" as the oxymoron it is: return is payment for risk, so exceptional promised returns *are* a risk disclosure, or a fraud disclosure.

## Insure catastrophes; self-insure annoyances — and hold a cash moat

Insurance has exactly one rational job: transferring risks *you couldn't absorb* — the house fire, the disability, the liability suit, the death of the earner with dependents. For those, insure fully and gladly overpay the expected value; that's what buying certainty costs, and ruin-avoidance is worth it ([[probabilistic-thinking]] — expected value logic stops at ruin). But the same industry sells coverage for risks you could trivially absorb — gadget warranties, tiny-deductible plans, insurance-shaped lottery tickets — where the premium markup is pure profit on your loss-aversion. The rule: **big deductibles, catastrophic coverage, self-insure everything below the "could I write this check without changing my life?" line.** The enabler of that rule is the **emergency fund** — months of expenses in boring, instantly-reachable cash. Its return isn't the interest; it's that every surprise stops being a crisis: the car repair becomes an inconvenience instead of credit-card debt, the job loss becomes a runway instead of a panic, and — quietly the biggest payoff — you're never *forced* to sell investments at the bottom because life demanded cash at the worst moment.

## The saboteur is in the mirror — build the machine accordingly

Long-run investor returns lag the very funds investors hold — because of *timing*: buying excited at peaks, selling terrified at troughs, reliably, decade after decade. Panic-selling in a crash converts a temporary decline into a permanent loss with a signature on it; the crash was the market's event, but the loss was a *decision*. The defenses are all structural, not motivational: automate contributions so they continue straight through the terrifying months (buying cheap while others flee); write your plan down *in calm times* — allocations, and what you'll do (nothing) when markets drop 30% — so future-you argues with a document instead of a mood ([[making-decisions]] — the decision journal, with money on it); check portfolios yearly, not daily (daily checking manufactures loss-aversion and itchy fingers from pure noise); and let every windfall sit **90 days** before major decisions — grief, bonuses, and inheritances are the natural habitat of the regrettable purchase. Boring, automated, and rare-touching isn't the compromise strategy — it *is* the sophisticated strategy; the excitement being sold to you elsewhere is, precisely, the product.
