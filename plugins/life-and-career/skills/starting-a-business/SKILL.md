# Starting a Business

Start with the base rate, because everything sensible follows from it: most new businesses fail, and the modal failure isn't bad execution of the idea — it's flawless execution of an idea *nobody needed* ([[probabilistic-thinking]] — the outside view first, always). That means the founder's primary job in the early days is not building, branding, or pitching; it's **driving down the probability that you're wrong about demand, as cheaply as possible**. Everything in this skill is a technique for buying that information at a discount ([[making-decisions]] — pay for information that changes the choice).

## Opportunities are problems wearing work clothes

Businesses are built on problems that are **painful** (people already spend money or hours on workarounds — the workaround is the tell; mild annoyances don't open wallets), **frequent** (recurring pain builds recurring revenue), and **reachable** (you can find and talk to the people who have it — a wonderful product for an audience you can't reach is a diary entry). The richest hunting grounds are asymmetries of knowledge you already own: the broken process at the industry you've worked in for years, the tool your niche community keeps wishing existed, the thing *you* built for yourself that others keep asking about. "Ideas" conjured in brainstorms compete with everyone; problems you've personally marinated in come with a decade of unfair context ([[navigating-your-career]] — the spike is also a periscope).

Note what's missing from the criteria: novelty. Markets with existing, profitable competitors are *validated* markets — competition proves the money exists. The fantasy of the competitor-free idea usually resolves to one of two realities: nobody's tried it, or everybody's tried it ([[evaluating-information]] — survivorship applies to ideas too; the graveyard doesn't advertise).

## Validate demand before building supply

The cardinal sequence error — months of building, then a launch into silence — comes from a natural confusion: building feels like progress and is comfortable; validation feels like rejection-risk and isn't ([[managing-yourself]] — that's emotion regulation masquerading as diligence). Invert it:

- **Interview the pain, not the idea.** Ask about *past behavior*, never hypothetical enthusiasm: "walk me through the last time this problem bit you — what did it cost? what did you try?" beats "would you use a product that…?" — because people are polite, futures are free, and compliments are the fool's gold of validation. The only answers that count describe money spent, time burned, and workarounds built ([[evaluating-information]] — revealed preference over stated preference, always).
- **Make the test costly to say yes to.** A pre-order, a deposit, a signed letter of intent, a waitlist that requires a card — each converts polite interest into evidence, because the currency of validation is *sacrifice*, not sentiment. Ten enthusiastic conversations and zero pre-orders is a validated *no*.
- **Sell it before it exists, ethically**: the landing page describing the product with a "buy" button that says "coming — join with a deposit" is a legitimate demand experiment when you're transparent about timelines. Concierge it next: deliver the outcome *manually* to the first customers (the "product" is you and a spreadsheet behind a curtain) — you learn what they actually need before automating any of it ([[implementing-changes]] — the walking skeleton, commercial edition).

## Distribution is half the business — the half that kills

The engineer's fallacy, and the artist's: build something great and the world finds it. The world finds nothing; **channels** find things, and the honest question to answer *before* building is "how, mechanically, will the thousandth customer hear this exists?" — with a real answer (a community you're already credible in, search demand you've measured, partnerships, content that compounds, sales calls you're willing to make), not "word of mouth" (a results-of-success, not a strategy) or "marketing" (a department, not an answer). Many experienced founders now choose *audience-first or channel-first*: pick the reachable audience, then find its burning problem — inverting the idea-first sequence precisely because reach is the scarcer asset. If you can't name the channel, the idea isn't ready regardless of how good the product would be ([[understanding-the-problem]] — "reachable" was in the acceptance criteria).

## Unit economics: the arithmetic that doesn't care about the story

Every business, stripped of its deck, is one sentence of arithmetic: *acquire a customer for less than the profit they generate, repeatably.* The literacy that separates businesses from expensive hobbies — **CAC** (the true, all-in cost of acquiring one customer — including your time, the founder subsidy everyone forgets), **contribution margin** (what one sale contributes after *its own* costs), **LTV** (margin × how long they stay — churn quietly rules this number), and the ratio between them with the payback period attached. Run the arithmetic *early, on projected honesty* ([[first-principles-thinking]] — price the raw materials of the model): some ideas are structurally doomed at any scale (CAC that can never fit inside thin margins), and better to meet that fact in a spreadsheet than in year two. Alongside it, the operating law: **cash is oxygen** — businesses die of cash timing, not just unprofitability (profitable-on-paper dies waiting on receivables), so know your runway in months and treat it as the one metric that never gets optimism applied ([[personal-finance-principles]] — the emergency fund logic, corporate edition).

## Start narrow, price on value, and climb the risk ladder

- **The niche is the wedge.** "Everyone" is not a market; the beachhead is a group specific enough to dominate — *aviation maintenance logs*, not *B2B software*. Small markets won specifically generate the references, iteration speed, and word-of-mouth that broad mediocrity never does; expansion comes later, from strength ([[scoping-and-estimation]] — the vertical slice, as strategy).
- **Price on value delivered, not cost incurred** — and almost certainly higher than your instinct, because underpricing is the default founder error: it signals low value, attracts the worst customers, and starves the margin that funds everything else. Raising prices is among the highest-ROI experiments a young business can run, and losing the customers who only stayed for cheapness is frequently a feature.
- **De-risk in rungs, not leaps.** The folklore founder bets everything on day one; the *actual* base rates favor the boring ladder — validate nights-and-weekends, get to first revenue, then meaningful traction, then the jump, with runway banked ([[making-decisions]] — ruin risk is judged by the tail; keep yourself in the game). Entrepreneurship's real skill isn't risk-taking; it's *risk-shaping* — moving the big bets later, after the information is in.

And the exit discipline, decided in advance because in-the-moment you will be the least reliable judge you've ever been ([[making-decisions]] — pre-commitment beats willpower): write down what evidence, by when, means *persist* — and what means the kinder verb, *stop*. Sunk years are spent identically whether you continue or not; the founders with long careers are the ones who treated each venture as one draw from a distribution, banked the compounding assets that survive any single failure — the skills, the reputation, the network, the customer understanding ([[navigating-your-career]]) — and lived to draw again.
