# Health Fundamentals

Health has the same structure as personal finance ([[personal-finance-principles]]): the evidence-backed core fits on an index card, has barely changed in decades, and is drowned out by an industry whose revenue requires you to believe it's complicated — because the fundamentals can't be sold twice. The card: *move most days, lift something a couple of times a week, sleep seven to nine hours, eat mostly whole foods with enough protein, don't smoke, drink little or nothing, keep the people you love close, and get the boring screenings.* Everything below is about actually doing that card, and defending it against the noise. (And the honest boundary: this is population-level reasoning — your body, conditions, and medications are a specific case, and specific cases get clinicians.)

## The hierarchy: nail the multipliers before the rounding errors

The health industry's attention pyramid is inverted from the evidence's. Supplements, superfoods, cold plunges, and optimal training splits are *rounding errors* debated loudly; sleep, regular movement, diet quality, smoking, and alcohol are *multipliers* practiced quietly. The failure mode has a recognizable shape: researching magnesium brands at 1am — the research session itself costing the sleep that dwarfs anything magnesium will ever do ([[prioritization-and-focus]] — the vital few, applied to biology). Before adopting any optimization, ask the bottleneck question ([[systems-thinking]]): *is this addressing my current limiting factor, or is it more interesting than my limiting factor?* For nearly everyone, the limiting factor is one of the boring five, and it's usually sleep.

## Movement: the closest thing to a miracle, dosed like one

If exercise were a pill, its effect profile — on mortality, mood, cognition, sleep, and nearly every chronic disease — would make it the best-selling drug in history. The evidence-backed prescription is humbler than fitness culture implies:

- **Cardio base**: brisk-walk-or-better most days; the biggest health returns come from moving from *nothing to something*, and the curve keeps paying (though at diminishing rates — [[making-decisions]]' catalog) up through a few hours weekly. Intensity mix matters less than showing up; [[endurance-training]] covers the performance version.
- **Strength twice weekly**: the most under-prescribed piece, and increasingly the important one with age — muscle and bone are a retirement account whose withdrawals start in your thirties unless deposits continue. A basic full-body routine is enough; the optimal program you'll quit loses to the decent one you'll keep ([[building-habits]] — friction is destiny).
- **The consistency law**: the difference between programs is small; the difference between *doing and not doing* is everything. Choose by what you'll still be doing in two years — which usually means choose what you can start tomorrow, near home, at a version that feels too easy ([[building-habits]] — the two-minute entry, in gym clothes).

## Sleep: the infrastructure everything else runs on

Sleep is not rest from life; it's when the maintenance runs — memory consolidation ([[learning-anything]] happens substantially asleep), emotional regulation ([[managing-yourself]]'s hardest days are disproportionately underslept days), immune function, and the hormonal machinery of appetite (short sleep reliably increases hunger — the diet fails at the pillow first). The engineering, in order of effect: **consistent wake time** (the single strongest lever — the body runs on rhythm, and weekend drift is self-inflicted jet lag), dark/cool/quiet room, caffeine as an 8-plus-hour drug (the 3pm coffee is a sleep decision), alcohol as sleep's counterfeit (sedation is not sleep architecture; it fragments the back half), and screens less for their light than for their *content* — the feed is engineered arousal at exactly the wrong hour. Chronic loud snoring or unrefreshing sleep despite the hygiene: that's a clinician conversation, not a discipline problem.

## Food: principles that survive the diet wars

Step back from the trench warfare and the major eating patterns that actually show long-term results share a boring core — which is the tell that the core, not the branding, is the active ingredient:

- **Mostly whole foods.** The dominant modern dietary problem isn't a macronutrient; it's ultra-processed engineering — food designed to be eaten past fullness. Cook more; shop the perimeter; treat ingredient lists like [[evaluating-information]] treats claims.
- **Enough protein**, at every age and doubly so when training or aging — it's the macro that protects muscle and satiety both.
- **Energy balance is physics; adherence is the game.** Every diet that works, works through eating less than you burn *in a way you can sustain*; the "best" diet is the one that fits your life well enough to still be running in year three ([[building-habits]] — same law, third appearance, because it's the law).
- **No demonized single foods, no sainted ones.** Patterns over ingredients; the diet is what you do weekly, not what you did today. Rigid food rules that generate shame-and-rebound cycles cost more than the foods did.

## Stress, connection, and the checkups you're postponing

Three items the fitness industry skips because they don't sell equipment: **Chronic stress is physiological**, not attitudinal — it erodes sleep, immunity, and judgment through named pathways, and its treatment is structural (load renegotiation, recovery scheduled like meetings, movement — [[managing-yourself]]). **Relationships are a health input** — social connection's effect on longevity rivals smoking's in the epidemiology, and the decades-long happiness studies keep returning the same headline: the quality of your close relationships is the best predictor there is. Budget for people like you budget for workouts. And **prevention is asymmetric**: the screenings, the vaccinations, the dental visits, the blood pressure check — boring, cheap, occasionally life-saving, and systematically postponed because nothing hurts yet ([[probabilistic-thinking]] — small annual probabilities compound over decades into near-certainties; catch them while they're cheap).

## Evaluating health claims: the standing gauntlet

No domain produces more confident nonsense per capita, because health anxiety is a renewable resource and everyone has a body to worry about. Run every claim through [[evaluating-information]] with these presets: *mechanism is not outcome* ("boosts metabolism" in a petri dish is not weight lost in a human); *mouse studies are about mice*; *"clinically proven" without a linked trial is a font choice*; supplements are presumed underwhelming until shown otherwise (the industry's regulatory bar is safety-ish, not efficacy) — with a handful of boring, well-supported exceptions your clinician can name for your case; and the seller's incentive question first, always. The strongest tell remains asymmetry: real findings arrive hedged and incremental; products arrive certain and revolutionary. When someone's certainty exceeds the evidence's, you're reading marketing ([[probabilistic-thinking]] — calibration applies to sellers too).
