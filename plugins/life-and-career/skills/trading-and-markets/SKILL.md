# Trading and Markets

This skill opens where every honest treatment must and almost no trading content does: **the base rates** ([[probabilistic-thinking]] — outside view first, especially when the inside view is exciting). Across decades of brokerage-account studies, day-trader cohorts, and regulator disclosures, the finding is brutally consistent: the large majority of active retail traders underperform simply holding the index, and most persistent day traders *lose money outright* — figures like "70–80% of retail CFD/forex accounts lose" appear on brokers' own legally-mandated disclosures. Meanwhile the boring alternative ([[personal-finance-principles]] — index, automate, ignore) compounds quietly for anyone who takes it. Any trading education that doesn't start here is marketing ([[evaluating-information]] — and note who profits from your trading volume regardless of your results: the broker, the platform, the course-seller. The incentive structure around retail trading is a machine for manufacturing overconfidence).

So the deepest skill here is mostly *defense* — and that's not a evasion; it's the expertise. What follows is what actually separates the surviving minority from the donation pool.

## The counterparty question: every trade needs a loser story

A trade is not a purchase; it's a *disagreement with a counterparty*. When you buy expecting a rise, someone sells expecting otherwise — and on the other side of retail trades, statistically, sit professional market makers and funds with faster data, better models, and thinner costs. So the qualifying question before every active position, the one that dissolves most of them: **"Who is on the other side of this trade, and why exactly are they wrong?"** ([[understanding-finance]] — everything public is already in the price; your reaction to the earnings headline *is the consensus*, not an insight). If the honest answer is "someone who hasn't watched the videos I've watched," you have located the fish at the table, and it's holding your account.

Real **edge** — the only justification for active trading — comes in a few auditable flavors: *informational* (you know something material first — mostly illegal or institutional), *analytical* (you process public information better — competing with armies of PhDs), *structural* (you can hold when others are forced to sell, take illiquidity others can't), or *behavioral* (you stay disciplined where others panic — the only edge realistically open to individuals, and the hardest to keep). The audit: write down which one you're claiming and what evidence supports it beyond recent results ([[thinking-like-a-scientist]] — falsifiable, in advance). "I've been up three months" is not evidence; in a bull market, everyone is a genius, and the tide's role in your swimming becomes visible only when it goes out.

## The behavioral failure modes: know thine enemy, it is thee

Markets are an adversarial environment *engineered by evolution's leftovers* to trigger your worst reflexes ([[managing-yourself]] — under real money stress, the calm reasoner leaves the room):

- **Loss aversion inverts discipline**: losses hurt roughly twice what gains please, so traders sell winners early (lock the pleasure) and ride losers down (avoid *realizing* the pain) — the exact opposite of every sound strategy. The market pays you to do what hurts.
- **Revenge trading**: after a loss, the urge to "make it back" *now*, with size — grief converted directly into position sizing. This is how bad days become account obituaries. The professional rule: a loss limit per day/week that, when hit, ends trading *by pre-commitment*, not by willpower at the moment of maximum tilt ([[making-decisions]] — decide in calm what you'll do in fire).
- **Overtrading**: activity feels like edge; the studies are unanimous that trading *more* correlates with earning *less* — every trade pays costs and spread, and most were impulses wearing analysis ([[prioritization-and-focus]] — the same lesson: motion isn't progress).
- **Narrative capture and FOMO**: the coin that 100x'd, the neighbor's gains, the mania du jour — by the time a trade is socially exciting, you are the exit liquidity ([[evaluating-information]] — survivorship: the losers of the mania aren't posting).

Every one of these is countered the same way: **rules written before the trade** — entry reason, exit conditions both directions, size — and a journal that grades you on *process adherence*, not outcome, because in a noisy game, good decisions and good results decouple constantly ([[making-decisions]] — the journal; [[probabilistic-thinking]] — resulting is the cardinal sin of judging decisions by single outcomes).

## Ruin math: sizing is survival

The arithmetic that governs everything and gets learned last: **losses and gains are asymmetric** — down 50% requires up 100% to recover; down 90% requires 900%. Deep drawdowns aren't setbacks; they're near-irreversible ([[understanding-finance]] — and leverage manufactures them from ordinary volatility, on the lender's clock, not yours). The consequences: position sizes such that *no single trade — and no plausible cluster of correlated ones* — can meaningfully dent the account (the old pros' 1–2% risk-per-trade rule is ruin math, not timidity); leverage treated as fire near dry tinder; and options understood before touched — buying them is mostly-expiring lottery tickets with a real pricing model working against you, selling them is collecting pennies before an occasional steamroller, and both have wiped out people who could recite these sentences. The meta-rule from [[making-decisions]]: positive expected value is *irrelevant* on paths that pass through zero — survival first, returns second, forever in that order.

## If you engage anyway: the tuition-capped apprenticeship

Curiosity about markets is legitimate — they're the most fascinating game humans have built, and there *is* real skill in the surviving minority. The honest structure for pursuing it: **wall it off** — a fixed, small percentage of investable assets (a common honest number: 5–10%) in a separate account, with the core compounding untouchably in the boring portfolio ([[personal-finance-principles]] — the machine runs regardless of your hobby's results); **treat it as tuition** — sized so total loss is affordable education, measured *against the index* (the only honest benchmark: if your trading account lags what the same money would've done sleeping in an index fund, the market has issued your grade); **paper trade first, but discount it** — mechanics transfer, psychology doesn't (no simulator teaches what real losses do to your hands — [[from-knowledge-to-judgment]]: stakes are the fixative, which is exactly why small real stakes beat large simulated ones); and **audit backtests like a hostile reviewer** ([[thinking-like-a-scientist]]): strategies fitted to history are astrology with parameters — twenty rules tried, the best one kept, is the forking-paths garden charging commission; demand out-of-sample results, costs included, and distrust anything whose seller retails a "system" that allegedly prints money (the question that never gets a good answer: *why is this for sale?*).

And keep one sentence taped where the charts are: **you don't have to play.** The index fund captures the market's whole long-run return for zero skill, zero hours, and zero adrenaline; active trading is a *voluntary* competition against the best-resourced players on earth, entered mostly by people who never checked the base rates. Entering with eyes open, capped stakes, written rules, and an honest scoreboard is a defensible hobby and occasionally a real education. Entering any other way, the market will teach the same curriculum — it just sets its own tuition ([[navigating-your-career]] — and the compounding assets are still the skills; even a losing tuition-capped year teaches probability, self-knowledge, and humility at prices cheaper than most MBAs).
