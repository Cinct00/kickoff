# Persuasion and Negotiation

Being right is not a strategy. Ideas don't win because they're correct; they win because someone moved them through other minds — and minds don't move the way facts suggest they should. The good news: persuasion and negotiation are learnable structures, not personality gifts, and the ethical versions *outperform* the manipulative ones wherever you'll ever deal with the same people twice — which is everywhere that matters. The line to hold: persuade with true things toward outcomes you'd defend openly; the moment you need the other side to misunderstand in order to win, you've started paying long-term reputation for short-term gain, at loan-shark rates.

## Positions are demands; interests are reasons — negotiate the reasons

The foundational move: beneath every position ("I need a 15% raise", "the deadline is non-negotiable") sits an interest — the *why* ("I feel underpaid relative to the new hires", "I promised the board a Q3 demo"). Positions can only be split; interests can often *both* be satisfied, because they're rarely actually opposed. The magic question, asked with real curiosity: **"help me understand what's driving that?"** The demo needs three features by Q3 — maybe two ship polished and one as a mockup, and both sides get more than the compromise would have given. Deadlocks are usually two positions grinding; the exit is almost always one level down.

This is also the heart of persuasion generally: people aren't blocked by missing facts nearly as often as by unaddressed interests — status, safety, workload, looking good to *their* boss. The pitch that fails "on the merits" usually failed on an interest nobody surfaced.

## People persuade themselves — build the conditions

Direct argumentative pressure produces resistance, not agreement: push a claim and watch the other mind generate counterarguments in real time (every "actually, you're wrong because..." makes the belief *stronger* — arguing is rehearsal). What moves minds:

- **Questions over assertions.** "What would have to be true for this to work?" and "how do you see the risk on X?" enlist their reasoning instead of opposing it — and conclusions people reach themselves, they own and defend.
- **Steelman aloud, first.** State their case so well they'd sign it ("so your concern is that we've tried this twice and paid for it both times — is that fair?"). Until someone feels *understood*, they allocate zero capacity to understanding you; after it, the same argument lands entirely differently. This costs nothing and is the single highest-leverage persuasion habit ([[difficult-conversations]] runs on the same engine).
- **Make it cheap to move.** Most public resistance is fear of losing face. Provide the ladder: "the situation's changed since that decision", "this new data wasn't available then" — let the mind change *without* the person having been wrong. Cornering someone into admitting defeat wins the argument and loses the decision.
- **Right size, right time.** Ask for the pilot, not the policy; catch people before positions harden in public. Pre-wiring the key people one-on-one before the big meeting isn't politics — it's letting persuasion happen where face isn't at stake.

## Your power is your BATNA — build it before you need it

Your **BATNA** — best alternative to negotiated agreement — is what you'll do if this deal dies, and it silently sets every number in the room. With a competing offer, "no" is calm information; without one, "no" is a bluff, and experienced counterparts smell the difference regardless of your acting. Consequences: *improve your alternatives before negotiating, not during* (interview elsewhere before the raise conversation; qualify a second vendor before the renewal); estimate **their** BATNA too, since the zone of possible deals lives between the two; and derive your **walk-away point** from your BATNA *in advance, in writing* — decided mid-negotiation, it will drift wherever the momentum pushes it. A negotiation you cannot walk away from is a petition.

## Expand the pie before dividing it, and trade asymmetries

Treating negotiation as pure division ("every dollar to you is a dollar from me") leaves value rotting on the table. Before haggling over the split, hunt for **asymmetric valuations** — things cheap for one side and precious to the other: flexibility on timing, payment terms, scope, publicity, future options, who takes which risk. "I can be flexible on start date if you can move on the base" routinely makes *both* sides better off than any midpoint on a single axis. The mechanism requires information, so **make multiple offers simultaneously** (three packages you value equally — their choice reveals what they prize) and never negotiate one issue at a time (single issues are pure division; bundles have trade-space). When division does come: anchor deliberately — first numbers gravitationally shape final ones, so open ambitious-but-defensible rather than waiting ([[probabilistic-thinking]] — the anchor bias works on you too; re-derive your number from fundamentals before responding to theirs). And concede *slowly, with reasons, getting something each time*: free concessions teach the counterpart to wait for more.

## Salary and offer negotiation: the playbook

The highest-stakes negotiation most people face, and the one most people skip. Employers expect it; a polite, well-reasoned counter almost never costs an offer.

**Timing**
- Build your BATNA before the conversation: other processes running, or a clear-eyed view of staying. Line up interviews to finish around the same time, so offers overlap.
- Deflect the number question early in the process: "I'd like to learn more about the role before talking numbers. What range is budgeted?" If pushed, give a researched range whose bottom is a number you'd be happy with.
- Never accept or counter on the phone call where the offer arrives. Thank them, show enthusiasm, ask for it in writing, and agree a reply date.

**Know the market**
- Sources: recruiters, peers at similar companies, salary sites, published bands, and offers from your own processes. Anchor on the role and level, not on your current pay.
- Re-derive your target from that research before you see their number, so their figure doesn't become your anchor.

**The package board**
Write every component down, because the base is only one lever and often the stiffest one:
- base salary, bonus target and how it actually paid out last year
- equity or long-term incentives (type, vesting, cliff, refresh policy)
- signing bonus (often the easiest to move, as it's one-off)
- pension contribution, holiday, notice period, remote or hybrid terms
- level or title (it sets your next raise and your next job's anchor)
- start date, learning budget, review date (e.g. a guaranteed six-month review)

**The counter**
- Lead with enthusiasm and a reason: "I'm excited about this. Based on [competing offer / market data / scope of the role], I was expecting closer to X. Is there flexibility?"
- Ask for a specific number slightly above your target, with a defensible basis.
- Then stop talking. Silence is part of the move.
- If base is fixed, trade across the board: "If base is capped, could we look at the signing bonus or the level?"
- Make one or two rounds, not five. End by confirming everything in writing.

**Scripts that work**
- On their range: "Thanks. Given [reason], I'd be looking at the top of that range."
- On an exploding deadline: "I want to give this a proper answer. Could we make it [date]?"
- With a competing offer: be truthful about it and specific about what matters ("the other offer is higher on base; I'd prefer to join you").

**Raises in your current job**
Make the case on impact and market, not on need. Bring your wins file, a market range and a specific ask, and do it ahead of the budget cycle, not after it ([[navigating-your-career]]). If the answer is no, ask what would need to be true for yes, and by when, and write it down.

## The relationship is the long game

Wherever you'll deal with the same people again — colleagues, vendors, bosses, the industry that talks to itself — every negotiation is one round of an iterated game, and your reputation is the compounding asset ([[systems-thinking]]: the trust stock fills slowly and drains fast). Squeezing the last 5% from someone with no alternatives is expensive victory: they remember, they hedge, they leave when they can, and they tell others. The durable operators leave something on the table on purpose, keep their word expensively, and treat the counterpart's good outcome as partly their problem — not from softness, but because the lifetime value of "good to deal with" dwarfs any single round's spoils. Hold firm on interests, generous on face, honest always: that combination, run for years, is what people call influence.
