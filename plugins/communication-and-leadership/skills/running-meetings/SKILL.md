# Running Meetings

A meeting is the most expensive artifact most organizations produce daily — eight people for an hour is a full person-day, spent in its most perishable form — and it's bought with the least scrutiny. The same company that reviews a $500 purchase lets a weekly hour with twelve attendees run for two years on inertia. Treat meeting time like the budget line it is, and most of what follows becomes obvious.

## First decide whether it should exist

Meetings have exactly a few legitimate jobs, and knowing which one you're buying determines everything else:

- **Deciding** — a real decision with the deciders present. The strongest reason to meet.
- **Creating** — working a problem together: designing, brainstorming ([[creative-thinking]] — with generation and judgment kept apart), incident triage.
- **Connecting** — trust, morale, the human fabric. Legitimate! But be honest that it's the purpose, and stop disguising it as status.
- **Informing** — and here's the audit: pure information transfer almost never needs synchrony. A written update reads in one-tenth the time it takes to sit through ([[technical-communication]] — done/next/blocked), and the classic status roundtable is N people waiting for their turn to talk while (N−1) check their laptops. Move it async and reclaim the hour.

The heuristic: **if nothing will be different after the meeting — no decision made, nothing created, no one changed — it was an email.** And for recurring meetings, run the quarterly zero-based audit: cancel it by default and see who asks for it back. The ask-backs are your real meetings; the silence is your refund.

## The agenda is a set of questions, and the invite list is a cost

Two design moves before anyone enters a room:

- **Frame agenda items as questions to answer, not topics to discuss.** "Q3 hiring" is an invitation to wander; "Do we backfill the data role or convert it to platform? (decide today)" tells everyone what done looks like and makes drift *visible* ([[understanding-the-problem]] — the same move, miniaturized). If you can't phrase the item as a question, you haven't figured out why it's on the agenda — which the meeting will discover expensively on your behalf.
- **Invite the minimum viable set.** Every added attendee raises the cost linearly and lowers the candor and airtime nonlinearly. Distinguish *needed* (decider, expertise, owner of the outcome) from *informed* (send them the notes — that's what notes are for). Big audiences are for announcing, not deciding; eight is already straining a decision meeting.

For decision meetings with real substance, steal the **document-first pattern**: a short pre-written memo ([[writing-clearly]]) read silently in the first minutes of the meeting itself — not sent ahead to be unread. Writing forces the proposer's thinking into checkable form; silent reading puts everyone at the same starting line; and discussion then begins from understanding instead of from the loudest first impression.

## Facilitate the airtime, not just the clock

An unfacilitated meeting is won by fluency and volume, not correctness — and the correlation between talking most and knowing most is roughly zero. Whoever runs the meeting owns the correction:

- **Draw out the quiet deliberately.** "Priya, you've run this system longest — what are we missing?" The silent expert usually has the load-bearing objection and no appetite for interrupting ([[leading-a-team]] — safety determines whether you ever hear it).
- **Break the anchor.** The first stated opinion drags every subsequent one toward it — especially when it's the senior person's ([[probabilistic-thinking]] — anchoring works in rooms too). For consequential judgments, collect positions *before* discussion: a silent minute of writing, votes revealed simultaneously. Cheap, and it reliably surfaces the disagreement that polite sequential discussion would have smoothed over — and the disagreement is the information ([[making-decisions]]).
- **Park tangents visibly.** The interesting-but-off-question thread goes on the parking lot list, with its owner, out loud. This honors the point without surrendering the agenda — the polite alternative to letting one tangent eat three questions.
- **Name the drift.** "We're relitigating the decision from item one — is there new information, or do we move on?" Somebody has to say it; the facilitator is paid in saved person-hours to be that somebody.

## Decisions need a decider; endings need owners and dates

The two failure points where good meetings quietly become expensive theater:

- **The undecided decision.** Discussion ends in warm nodding, everyone leaves with a different impression of what was agreed, and the topic returns next week wearing a new hat. The fix is structural: every decision-question has a **named decider** ([[making-decisions]] — separate the decider from the input-givers), and the meeting ends with the decision *stated in words*, out loud, ugly and explicit: "Decision: we backfill. Sasha decides the level by Friday." Consensus theater feels kinder; the third relitigation isn't.
- **The ownerless action.** "We should really update the runbook" is a wish, not an action, and wishes don't ship. Every action item leaves the room with **an owner (one name, not a team) and a date** — and the last two minutes of the meeting are for reading the list aloud, which is precisely when the phantom items ("wait, I can't do that by Tuesday") get caught cheaply.

Then write the note — decisions, owners, dates, five sentences — and send it to attendees *and* the merely-informed within the hour ([[technical-communication]]). The note is what makes the meeting exist in the organizational record; an unwritten decision decays into N private recollections, and next month's argument about what was decided.

## Respect the maker schedule

One last cost the calendar hides: for anyone doing deep work, a meeting doesn't cost its duration — it costs the duration *plus the shattered focus block around it* ([[prioritization-and-focus]] — attention residue). A 30-minute meeting at 10:30 can delete a morning. So: batch meetings into corridors (afternoons, meeting-day blocks) and defend contiguous maker time as a team norm; default to 25/50 minutes so back-to-backs breathe; and start on time regardless of stragglers — waiting five minutes for latecomers taxes the punctual to subsidize the late, and teaches everyone the real start time is fiction. The meeting culture you tolerate is the meeting culture you have.
