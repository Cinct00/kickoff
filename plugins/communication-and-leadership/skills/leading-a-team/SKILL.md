# Leading a Team

The promotion nobody explains: the skills that got you here now work against you. As an individual contributor, your instinct to grab the hard problem and solve it was the job; as a lead, that same instinct *starves your team of growth and buries you in work only you can't delegate anymore*. The new job has a different output metric — not what you produce, but what the team produces, including what it produces after you stop watching. Everything below follows from that one inversion.

## Delegate outcomes, and don't take the monkey back

Weak delegation hands out *tasks* ("run these three queries") and keeps the thinking; real delegation hands out **outcomes** ("find out why enterprise churn spiked — you own the answer") and keeps only the safety net. Task-delegation feels safer and caps the team at the resolution of your own instructions — you become the bottleneck through which all thinking must pass ([[systems-thinking]] would point out you've built a hub-and-spoke and are surprised the hub is tired).

Two disciplines make outcome-delegation work:

- **Match authority to responsibility.** Owning the outcome without owning the decisions ("you're responsible, but check everything with me") is accountability theater, and good people leave over it. Define the envelope explicitly — budget, deadline, what needs sign-off — then respect it, including when they choose differently than you would have. *Especially* then: a decision overridden for taste (not correctness) teaches everyone the ownership was fiction.
- **Refuse the returning monkey.** "How should I handle X?" is the delegated problem attempting to climb back onto your shoulders, and every time you answer with a solution, you teach the team that thinking is your job. Answer with the question instead: "what are the options you're weighing?" — coach the deciding, don't do it ([[teaching-and-explaining]] — productive struggle, applied to adults). Expect delegated work to be done 80% as well as you'd do it at first; that gap is the tuition for having a team that scales, and it closes.

## Lead with context, not control

Every decision you make *for* the team requires your presence forever; every piece of context you give the team keeps deciding *without* you. So invest where it compounds: make sure everyone knows **why** the work matters, what the customer feels, what the constraints and priorities actually are, how success is measured — and then be repetitive about it, because the message you're bored of saying is roughly when it's starting to land. A team rich in context makes a hundred small decisions a week correctly without asking; a team poor in context either queues at your door or guesses. When someone makes a call you'd never make, check the context supply before the person: nine times out of ten they were missing a fact, not a faculty ([[systems-thinking]] — diagnose the incentives and information flows before the character).

The same logic governs your calendar: **your time allocation is your real strategy document**, whatever the slide deck says. The team watches what you attend, ask about, and celebrate — and infers priorities from that with brutal accuracy. Audit it monthly against what you claim matters.

## The 1:1 is their meeting, and safety is your job

The recurring 1:1 exists for exactly the things that don't survive group settings: the career hope, the brewing frustration, the "I'm underwater," the thing they're seeing that you're not. Which means: **it is not a status meeting** — status has cheaper channels ([[running-meetings]]) — and it's not your agenda. Open with their items, ask the questions that don't get asked ("what's harder than it should be?", "what would you change about how we work?", "where do you want to be in two years?"), and then do the hard part: *shut up and let the silence work*. Cancel 1:1s routinely and you've announced, precisely, how much the person matters.

What determines whether truth shows up — in 1:1s and everywhere — is **psychological safety**: the team's learned answer to "what happens here to people who admit mistakes, ask basic questions, and disagree with the lead?" This is the strongest known predictor of team performance, and it is set almost entirely by *your visible reactions in small moments*: how you respond to the first bad news ([[technical-communication]] — if bad news travels slowly to you, you taught it to), whether you say "I was wrong" at conversational volume, whether the messenger of the production bug gets thanked or grilled ([[incident-response]] — blameless isn't just for postmortems). Praise publicly, correct privately, and disagree with ideas in ways that leave their owners intact ([[difficult-conversations]]).

## Motivation is mostly subtraction

Sustained drive runs on three fuels — **autonomy** (real ownership, see delegation), **mastery** (visible growth, problems slightly beyond current reach — the edge where learning happens, assigned deliberately), and **purpose** (the context above: who this helps and why it matters). Notice what's absent from the list: pressure, perks, and pep talks. The practical implication is that a leader's motivational work is mostly *removing demotivators* — the pointless process, the flip-flopping priorities, the credit misallocation, the meeting that eats the maker schedule — rather than adding rah-rah. A team of decent people with ownership, growth, and a reason will motivate itself; no speech survives contact with a purposeless backlog.

The subtler halves of the job: **distinguish shielding from hiding** — absorb the organizational churn and politics so the team can work (shielding), but never filter the strategic truth about where the company or project actually stands (hiding — they always find out, and what dies is your credibility); and **mind your amplification** — your idle musing arrives downstream as a directive, your bad mood as a weather system. Leads are loud even when whispering; calibrate accordingly.

## Hiring: the highest-leverage hours you'll spend

A single hire will contribute thousands of hours of work; the few hours of evaluation deserve rigor accordingly ([[making-decisions]] at full strength). The principles that survive contact: hire for **slope over intercept** where you can afford ramp time (trajectory and learning speed compound; current knowledge depreciates); define what "great in this role" means *before* interviewing, and structure interviews to sample it — unstructured conversation measures charm and similarity-to-you, then calls it culture fit; make interviews *work-shaped* (realistic problems over puzzle trivia); weigh evidence against your biases knowingly (the confident interviewer and the good engineer are different populations — [[evaluating-information]]); and **never average a hire out of a strong no** — a panel's lukewarm "fine" plus one investigated, articulate concern usually means no. The team you'll lead in two years is being decided in this loop; it outweighs everything else on your calendar this week.
