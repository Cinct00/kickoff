# Presenting and Public Speaking

A presentation is not information transfer — documents do that better, cheaper, and skimmably ([[running-meetings]]). What a live talk buys, at high cost, is *change*: attention directed, emphasis felt, a room moved from one understanding to another. Design for that or send the memo. And the craft is far more learnable than the naturals make it look — nearly everything that reads as "born speaker" is structure and rehearsal wearing a relaxed face.

## Design backward from one sentence

Before any slide exists, finish this: *"If the audience remembers exactly one sentence next week, it is ___."* ([[writing-clearly]] — the same law, spoken.) One sentence, not three — the talk that tries to land three messages lands zero, because audience memory is a small cup and you're pouring with a bucket. Everything in the talk either drives that sentence home or dilutes it; the fascinating tangent that doesn't serve it is cut, mourned briefly, and saved for another talk.

Then profile the actual audience before building anything ([[teaching-and-explaining]] — start where *they* are): What do they already know — so you neither re-teach the basics nor sail over heads? What do they *care* about — the executive hears risk and money where the engineer hears architecture, and the same content needs different clothes? What's their likely objection — address it before they raise it, and you own the room; ignore it, and it sits in every head, blocking everything after slide two.

## Structure as tension, not inventory

The default structure — an inventory of everything you know, in the order you learned it — is complete, orderly, and unmemorable. Human attention runs on **tension and release**: a question that wants answering, a problem that wants solving, a "before" that demands an "after." Build on that spine:

1. **Open with the gap, not the outline.** The first ninety seconds decide whether minds stay in the room. "Last March, our biggest customer almost left, and the reason surprised us" beats "Today I'll cover three topics" in every room ever convened. Agenda slides spend the most valuable attention of the talk on a table of contents.
2. **Alternate what-is and what-could-be** — problem and resolution, question and answer — in waves, each wave advancing the one message. The rhythm keeps attention renewed the way pure exposition never does.
3. **Concretize relentlessly.** One real customer, one actual failure, one specific number outpersuades any abstraction ([[writing-clearly]] — pictures over fog). Data convinces; *stories about the data* are what get retold — and getting retold is the actual win condition of a talk.
4. **End on the message, on purpose.** The last thirty seconds are the highest-retention real estate you have. Spending them on "so, yeah, that's basically it, any questions?" is burning the best slot on filler. Script the close word-for-word: restate the one sentence, name the ask, stop.

## Slides serve the speech — you are the presentation

The deadliest confusion in corporate life: the deck as teleprompter, walls of bullets read aloud to people who read faster than you speak. The audience can listen to you or read the slide — not both — so dense slides guarantee they're doing neither well. The disciplines:

- **One idea per slide**; a slide is an *exhibit* — a chart, an image, a single assertion in large type — not a paragraph. If a slide needs sub-bullets, it's two slides or it's speech.
- **Give every chart a headline that states its finding.** "Q3 Revenue" makes the audience do the analysis; "Churn concentrated in accounts without onboarding" *is* the analysis ([[evaluating-information]] — and mind your own axes; a truncated y-axis in your favor will be noticed by exactly the person you most need to convince).
- **The deck-as-document goes separately.** If people need a self-standing reference, write one ([[technical-communication]]) — don't average the two purposes into slides that fail at both.
- **Plan for the demo to fail.** Anything live will, at the worst moment, in front of the most senior person (the demo gods are real and petty). Record the backup video; screenshot the key states; rehearse the recovery sentence.

## Rehearse aloud — the only rehearsal that counts

Reading slides silently is not rehearsal; the mouth finds problems the eye forgives — the unpronounceable sentence, the transition that doesn't exist, the section that's twice its budgeted length. The regimen that separates polished from winged: **three full run-throughs, aloud, standing, timed.** First run is always terrible (that's what it's for); by the third, transitions carry themselves. Over-script two moments only — the opening ninety seconds (delivered on pure adrenaline, when your working memory is at its worst) and the close — and keep the middle as *practiced structure*, not memorized prose: recited talks die the moment one sentence goes missing, while structured talks bend and recover.

Time it honestly, then **cut 10%** — live delivery always runs long, and finishing early is a gift while running over steals from Q&A, the next speaker, and your close ([[scoping-and-estimation]] — the same optimism, the same tax). If you can, rehearse once in front of one honest human ([[difficult-conversations]] — and believe their frown over your intentions).

## Nerves are fuel mislabeled, and Q&A is a feature

Stage fright's dirty secret: the physiology of anxiety and of excitement are nearly identical — racing heart, sharpened senses — and the label is somewhat yours to choose. "I'm excited" outperforms "calm down" in the research because it works *with* the arousal instead of against it. The rest of the treatment is preparation (nerves feed on uncertainty, and the scripted opening exists precisely to carry you through the worst ninety seconds), plus the merciful truth that **audiences want you to succeed** — they're not auditing you; they're hoping you're good, because the alternative is boredom. And the visible confidence trick that's actually a competence trick: slow down. Nervous pace reads as nervous; deliberate pauses read as command — and give the audience time to think, which the talk was for.

For Q&A: **repeat or rephrase each question** (the room didn't hear it; you buy five seconds of thought; you flush misunderstandings before answering the wrong question). Answer the question asked — concisely, then stop; the three-minute answer to a one-line question is where credibility drains. Meet the hostile question with steelmanned grace ("the fair challenge in there is X — here's how we see it" — [[persuasion-and-negotiation]]); the room judges the temperature of your response far more than its content. And say **"I don't know — I'll find out and follow up"** without flinching: delivered plainly, it raises credibility, because everyone has watched the alternative, and nobody believed it.
