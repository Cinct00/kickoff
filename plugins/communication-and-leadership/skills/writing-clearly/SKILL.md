# Writing Clearly

Clear writing is not decoration on top of thinking — it *is* thinking, made inspectable. Muddy prose is almost always muddy thought: the sentence won't come out right because you haven't yet decided what it says. This is why writing feels hard and why it's worth doing even when nobody will read it: the page is where you discover what you actually believe. Everything below serves one master — the reader's limited, revocable attention.

## Know the one thing you're saying

Before drafting, finish this sentence: *"The point of this piece is ___."* One sentence, not three. If you can't, you're not ready to write — you're ready to think, and freewriting a messy page to *find* the point is a legitimate way to do that; just don't ship the finding process as the product. Everything in the final piece either serves the point or leaves. Documents with three main points have zero.

## Draft fast, edit slow — and never simultaneously

Writing and editing are opposite mental modes: generation wants momentum and tolerance for garbage; editing wants cold judgment. Running both at once — polishing each sentence before writing the next — is the mechanism of both writer's block and bloated prose. So split them ruthlessly: **draft** at speed, badly, without touching the backspace key more than necessary, until the shape exists; *then* **edit** in a separate sitting, ideally after enough delay that the text reads as someone else's ([[creative-thinking]] runs on the same separation). The quality of a piece is determined in editing; the *existence* of a piece is determined by protecting the draft from the editor.

## Structure for the impatient reader

Readers don't owe you their attention past the first paragraph, and most decide there. So: **the point goes first** — conclusion, then support; the inverted pyramid ([[technical-communication]] applies this to engineering artifacts). Then, within the piece:

- **One idea per paragraph**, opened by a sentence that states it. A reader skimming only first sentences should still get the argument — test this on your own draft; it's brutal and accurate.
- **Signpost the turns.** "But", "so", "despite this" are steering inputs; a paragraph that changes direction without one throws the reader off the vehicle.
- **End on purpose.** The last line is what echoes — spend it on the point or the action you want, never on trailing administrivia.

## Write concrete, write active, write short

The three edits that fix most prose:

1. **Concrete beats abstract.** "Improve operational efficiency" is fog; "cut invoice processing from five days to one" is a picture. Every abstraction in a draft is a place where a specific example is hiding — go find it. Readers remember pictures; they forgive but forget fog.
2. **Actors act.** "It was decided that the launch would be delayed" — by whom? Passive voice isn't a grammar sin; it's an *information deletion* (usually of responsibility), and nominalizations ("make a decision", "conduct an analysis") embalm verbs the same way. "Legal delayed the launch" — shorter, truer, and someone's name is on it.
3. **Plain words for hard ideas.** Complex vocabulary signals insecurity, not intelligence; the confident expert says "use" not "utilize", "start" not "commence". Save the reader's effort for the ideas — that's what it's budgeted for.

None of this means short sentences forever — monotone rhythm is its own fog. Vary length; let a short one land the punch after a long one builds it.

## Cut until it bleeds, then stop

Nearly every draft improves when cut 10–25%. The reliable targets: throat-clearing openers ("It is worth noting that..."), intensifiers doing nothing ("very", "really", "quite"), hedges beyond the honest ones ("I think that perhaps it might"), redundant pairs ("each and every"), and — hardest — the paragraph you love that serves a different essay. Cutting isn't loss; every deleted sentence raises the concentration of the ones that remain. The discipline has a limit: cut *fat*, not voice, and not the examples — under deadline pressure, writers cut the concrete details first, which is backwards; cut the abstractions and keep the pictures.

## Read it aloud, then read it as them

Two final passes catch what silent rereading can't:

- **Aloud** (or subvocalized slowly): your ear catches the clunks, the accidental rhymes, the sentence you can't finish in one breath, the word repeated three times in two lines. If you stumble reading it, the reader stumbles harder.
- **As the reader**: cold, wearing their knowledge, not yours. Where do they not know a term you didn't define ([[teaching-and-explaining]] — the curse of knowledge writes half of all bad prose)? What question forms at paragraph two that you don't answer until page four? What might they *misread* — and is the ambiguous sentence worth the risk? For anything high-stakes, an actual test reader beats both passes: watch where they frown, and believe the frown over your intentions.
