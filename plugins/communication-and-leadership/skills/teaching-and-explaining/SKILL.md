# Teaching and Explaining

Explanations fail for a reason so universal it has a name: the **curse of knowledge**. Once you understand something, you lose access to what it was like not to — the jargon feels like plain speech, the leaps feel like single steps, the prerequisites feel like common knowledge. Every teaching technique in this skill is, at bottom, a workaround for this one bug: you cannot trust your own sense of what's obvious, so you must engineer around it.

## Start from where they are, not where you are

Understanding is built, not transmitted: new ideas only stick when attached to something already in the learner's head. So the first move of any explanation is reconnaissance — a question or two: *what's your current picture of this? have you worked with anything similar?* Thirty seconds of this changes everything downstream: the analogy you pick, the vocabulary you're allowed, the step size you can take. Explaining without it is throwing a rope without checking where the other person is standing.

Then **anchor to what they know**: "a database index is like a book's index" lands instantly for anyone who's used one. Bridges beat foundations — you rarely need to build from zero, only to connect to the nearest thing they already have. (And when the anchor is imperfect, say where it breaks: every analogy has a cliff, and marking it costs one sentence.)

## Example first, abstraction second

The expert's instinct is to lead with the general principle — that's how the knowledge is stored *after* understanding. Learners need it in the order understanding actually arrives: **concrete case, then the pattern, then the general rule.** Show the specific email getting marked as spam, then how the filter scored it, then the abstraction. One well-chosen example beats three paragraphs of definition; two examples plus a counterexample ("this looks similar but *isn't* one, because...") beat almost anything — the counterexample is where the concept's boundary lives, and the boundary is what learners actually lack.

## One idea at a time, at their step size

Working memory is small and yours is pre-loaded with the material; theirs isn't. The failure mode is the *firehose* — covering everything because it's all connected and all interesting. Discipline: one core idea per explanation, ruthlessly; the fascinating tangents go in a parking lot for later. Split every leap that feels like one step to you into the three steps it actually is — if they look lost, the step was too big, not the learner too slow. And **welcome the "stupid" question as instrumentation**: it's the only honest signal of where the floor actually is, and how you receive the first one determines whether you ever get a second ([[difficult-conversations]] — safety is a prerequisite, not a nicety).

## Check by generation, never by nods

"Does that make sense?" is a politeness ritual — it measures social comfort, not comprehension, and it returns "yes" unconditionally. Recognition feels like understanding from the inside too ([[learning-anything]] — the fluency illusion), so the learner's own nod is sincere *and* worthless. The only trustworthy check is **generation**: have them produce something with the idea —

- "Walk me through how you'd apply this to ___" (a case you haven't covered)
- "Explain it back in your own words" (framed as checking *your* explanation, which is even true)
- "What would happen if ___?" (a prediction the idea should power)

Their errors are the curriculum: a wrong answer shows you the exact broken piece of their model, which is worth ten correct answers. Fix the piece, not the whole lecture.

## Let them struggle — briefly, productively

The teacher's hardest discipline is not rescuing. Watching someone grope toward an answer you could hand them in five seconds is uncomfortable, but the groping is where learning happens — answers given too early are rented, not owned. Calibrate: **productive struggle** (they have traction, hypotheses forming) gets silence or at most a question — "what have you ruled out?"; **unproductive struggle** (flailing, no traction, frustration compounding) gets a *hint at the smallest useful size* — point at the region, not the answer ("look closely at what happens on the boundary"). The graduated-hint habit is most of what makes great tutors great, and it's learnable.

## Teach the map, and the why

Facts without structure fall through; give learners the **schema** to hang them on — "there are three basic approaches, and today is the first" beats diving in cold ([[researching-a-topic]] maps before digging for the same reason). Attach *why it matters* before *how it works*: motivation is load-bearing, and "here's the problem this solves — here's what breaks without it" turns passive reception into active want. It's also honest to simplify: teach the clean 90%-true version first and refine later ("lies to children" is the fond name for this — every intro course does it on purpose); just flag it — "this is the simplified version; the edges get weirder" — so later refinement lands as depth, not betrayal.

And the meta-point that makes teaching worth volunteering for: **explaining is the most demanding retrieval practice there is** ([[learning-anything]]). Every gap in your own understanding announces itself the moment you try to explain past it. Teach the thing to learn the thing — the learner is not the only beneficiary in the room.
